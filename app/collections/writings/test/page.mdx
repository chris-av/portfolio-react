# On Building Things

There's a particular satisfaction that comes from shipping something real — a deployed app, a working pipeline, a tool that saves someone an hour a week. It doesn't have to be clever. It just has to work.

## The Trap of Perfect Architecture

Most engineers I know, myself included, have fallen into this trap at least once: spending two weeks designing the ideal system for a problem that could have been solved in two days.

> Architecture is only as good as the problem it solves. Over-engineer and you're solving problems you don't have yet.

The irony is that the "perfect" architecture often becomes a liability. You maintain abstractions that don't serve real needs, and when requirements change — and they always do — the rigidity works against you.

### When to Resist the Urge

Some signals that you're over-engineering:

- You're building for a scale you've never seen
- The abstraction layer has more code than the feature itself
- You can't explain the design to a teammate in five minutes

## Shipping as a Skill

Shipping consistently is a skill, not a personality trait. It's learnable. It requires knowing what "done" actually means for a given piece of work, and resisting the pull toward one more refactor before the PR goes up.

The engineers I've learned the most from aren't always the most technically brilliant. They're the ones who ship, gather signal, and iterate.
